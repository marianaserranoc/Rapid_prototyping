## Persona 1: The Package-Wary Tryer

### What's being tested
Whether the barrier was commitment risk or price-vs-gym comparison.

### The card shown
Off-peak slot, one single-class price set to match what the person already pays per gym visit — no package, no "buy 10 and save."

### How it ran
One team member showed the card to 8 people they knew who already have a gym membership (recruited the same way G1 was — friends of friends), one at a time, over a couple of days. Asked "would you book this?" then "why / why not?"

### Results (fictional)

| Shown the card | Said "I'd book it" | Said "I'd skip it" |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 5 | 3 |

**Why they said skip (noted on the spot):** 2 of the 3 said they'd still rather go to the gym since it's already paid for and "sure"; 1 said they didn't know the instructor and that mattered more than the price.

### Reading the result
5 of 8 said they'd book at gym-equivalent pricing — a decent lean toward price being the fixable part. But the one "I don't know the instructor" answer is a reminder this card only tested price; it didn't touch trust at all, so it can't fully separate the two.

### What this does and doesn't resolve
- **Supports:** worth pursuing gym-equivalent pricing further.
- **Does not resolve:** whether trust/instructor-familiarity is a second, separate lever — one answer hinted at it, but the card wasn't designed to test it.

### Cross-reference
See `gym_goers.md`, Persona 1 evidence table, "A full-price-refund offer would have changed the Friday decision" — a different, untested mechanism from this one.

## Persona 2: The Uncommitted Last-Minute Booker

### What's being tested
Whether commitment risk or booking friction drives the decision.

### The two cards shown
- **Card A:** the slot and price, plus the line "tap book and you're confirmed instantly."
- **Card B:** the same slot and price, plus the line "full refund if you don't love it."

### How it ran
One team member showed Card A to 5 people and Card B to a different 5 people (same recruiting pool as G2 — people with unpredictable schedules who sample classes occasionally), asked "would you book this?", noted the answer and why.

### Results (fictional)

| Card | Said "I'd book it" | Said "I'd skip it" |
|---|---|---|
| A — instant confirm | 4 | 1 |
| B — refund guarantee | 2 | 3 |

**Why they said skip (Card B):** two of the three said some version of "I'd still want to know I'm actually getting in before I plan my morning around it" — i.e., the refund doesn't help if the booking itself feels unconfirmed.

### Reading the result
4 of 5 vs. 2 of 5 — small numbers, but the reasoning behind the "skip" answers is the more useful part: people on Card B weren't worried about losing money, they were worried about not knowing if the class was actually theirs. That's a friction/certainty concern, not a risk concern — which lines up with G2's own episode (she gave up after a slow reply, not after worrying the class would be bad).

### What this does and doesn't resolve
- **Supports:** instant confirmation addresses something a refund guarantee doesn't — certainty, not just risk.
- **Does not resolve:** whether combining both would do even better — not asked.

### Cross-reference
See `gym_goers.md`, Persona 2 evidence table, "Regret/uncertainty-aversion is the dominant driver" — the stated reasons here point toward friction/certainty over risk-aversion, though on a very small sample.

## Persona 3: The Unanchored Sampler

### What's being tested
Whether a low-commitment offer changes the decision at all, for someone with no fixed studio.

### The card shown
The off-peak slot, price, plus "full refund if you don't come back within 2 weeks."

### How it ran
Shown to 7 people who fit the profile (no fixed studio, tries different places), one at a time. Asked "would you book this?" and, if yes, "would you actually come back, or is this a one-time thing?"

### Results (fictional)

| Shown the card | Said "I'd book it" | Of those, said "I'd probably come back" |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | 5 | 3 |

### Reading the result
5 of 7 said yes to booking — a strong lean that the offer itself is appealing. 3 of those 5 said they'd likely return, which is closer to what the team actually cares about (repeat attendance, not just a first booking).

### What this does and doesn't resolve
- **Supports:** worth a slightly bigger next round.
- **Does not resolve:** this is all stated intention ("I'd probably come back"), not observed behavior — nobody in this card-based test actually booked or returned for real. That's the honest limit of this method: it tells you what people say they'd do, not what they'd do.

### Cross-reference
See `EXAMPLE-interview-goer3.md` (Example-G3), Q4–Q6, on price sensitivity at the booking step.

## Cross-persona summary

| Persona | Card tested | Said "I'd book it" | Headline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Package-Wary Tryer | Gym-equivalent price | 5 of 8 | Price looks fixable; one answer hinted trust also matters |
| 2 — Uncommitted Last-Minute Booker | Instant confirm vs. refund guarantee | 4 of 5 vs. 2 of 5 | Certainty/friction beat risk-reduction in stated reasons |
| 3 — Unanchored Sampler | Refund guarantee | 5 of 7 | Appealing offer; return intent is only stated, not observed |

**What this method actually is, worth being upfront about:** this is a stated-preference test, not a behavioral one — people are saying what they'd do with a card in front of them, not actually booking and showing up. That's honest, fast, and cheap, which is the whole point at this stage — but it's a weaker signal than a real booking would be, and the natural next step (once a card-tested idea looks promising) is to make it bookable for real, even manually, and see if stated intent matches what people actually do.
